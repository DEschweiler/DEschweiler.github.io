Reassessing established reference values for morphometry on radiographs

Reference values that were established decades ago on few patients may not fully mirror what is seen in everyday clinical routine. A generalist framework for musculoskeletal measurements on radiographs puts them to the test on tens of thousands of images.

DetailsClose
  • The framework I developed is generalist. Landmarks are placed once on a reference radiograph and transferred to every other radiograph automatically, so it is not tied to a single measurement or anatomy.
  • On more than 53,000 knee radiographs, we already identified a concerning discrepancy between the traditional reference values and clinical routine for one measurement, the Insall-Salvati ratio.
  • Next, we plan to extend this to other anatomies and projections to bring real benefit to healthcare and to the understanding of human anatomy.
  • We are also extending the framework to 3D to include MRI and CT, and pursue the same contributions there.

Segmentation and image generation for microscopy image data

A segmentation model can become an expert for your microscope without anyone annotating a single image. Image generation models make this possible by turning simulated cells into realistic, fully annotated microscopy images.

DetailsClose
  • Segmentation means outlining every cell in a microscopy image. Doing this on a robust and general level is hard, because you need many annotated examples that cover different microscopes, imaging techniques and quality standards.
  • Simulating cell shapes is much easier, and a simulated shape is already its own annotation. A cell shape simulator can also be reused once it is set up, which makes the system sustainable.
  • What is still missing is the image that belongs to each shape. An image generation model adds it by turning the simulated cell into a synthesized microscopy image, which gives fully annotated image and mask pairs for training segmentation approaches.
  • To adapt this to a new microscope setup, you simply image your cells, train a diffusion model on them without supervision and let it turn simulated cells into cells that look exactly like the ones you just imaged.

A first step towards robots that assist medical staff

Radiation is still a problem for medical staff. Robots could take over the tasks that expose clinicians to it.

DetailsClose
  • Robots are becoming omnipresent, and AI has reached a stage where they could advance quite a lot. As an engineer, I am particularly excited about this potential.
  • We work towards this with a general system that lets a robot map a room, understand what is in it and manipulate objects. A robot could, for example, find and unpack sterile tools and materials or monitor radiation in an angiography suite, to assist medical staff with tasks during radiation exposure.

Natural-language control of clinical software

Clinical software can be operated simply by saying what you need. Computer-use agents take over mouse and keyboard and work on the interface exactly as a human does.

DetailsClose
  • The agents only work with what is on the screen, so no programmatic access to the software is needed.
  • This opens up a lot of potential that we are working towards. Repetitive tasks can be automated, clinical staff can operate a system without touching it, even in sterile environments, and a user who is stuck can be shown how the system works, just like a colleague would show you.
